WhatsApp sales pipeline: how to define stages your team will actually use

Most pipelines die for the same reason: nobody knows when a customer should move to the next column. Fix that and the board starts telling you where sales slip away.

LVBy Lucas Vaz·November 2, 2026·7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Every stage needs an entry rule based on a fact in the conversation, not a salesperson's hunch.
  • Five or six stages are enough for most businesses; more turns into paperwork.
  • Every lost card needs a reason picked from a short list.
  • Three simple divisions each week show which stage is leaking sales.

A WhatsApp sales pipeline looks simple until week three. At first everybody drags the cards around. Then the "Interested" column becomes a dumping ground with 140 names, half of them people who asked about opening hours back in August, and nobody trusts the board anymore.

The tool is rarely the problem. The stages were given names but no rules. Each salesperson decides on their own when someone counts as "interested," and you end up with a board that measures nothing.

If you don't have a board yet, start with the simple four-column routine for turning conversations into sales. This piece is the next step: defining stages with clear criteria so the pipeline tells the truth.

What makes a pipeline stage useful

A useful stage has three things: a name everyone understands, an entry rule and an exit rule. The rule has to be a fact you can see in the conversation, not a feeling.

"Customer seems excited" is not a rule. "Customer gave us the event date" is. "We sent the quote" is. "Customer confirmed they want to book" is.

Why does this matter so much? Because a pipeline is only useful for comparing numbers week to week. If Julia puts anyone who asked for a price into Quote, and Ryan only moves people who received a PDF with figures, your close rate changes depending on who answered, and you will draw the wrong conclusions.

One more rule that saves a lot of pain: each card sits in one stage only and always has a next step with a date. A card without a date is a forgotten card.

How to set entry and exit rules for each stage

Here is a generic template that works for most businesses that sell through conversations. Use it as a starting point and rename the stages in your team's own words.

StageEnters whenLeaves when
New leadFirst message arrivedSomeone replied and understood the request
QualifiedWe know what they want and that we can serve itReceived a price or proposal
Quote sentPrice, quote or reservation sentSaid yes, said no or went quiet
NegotiationAsked to adjust price, timing or termsClosed or gave up
WonPaid, signed or booked with a depositDone (moves to after-sales)
LostDeclined or silent after the last follow-upMay come back as a new lead

Notice two details. First, "Qualified" requires knowing whether you can serve the request. Someone looking for a service you don't offer is not a qualified lead; it's a conversation to close politely. Second, "Lost" has an objective rule for people who disappear: after the last planned follow-up, the card gets out of the way. Without that, Quote sent turns into a graveyard.

A technical reminder: if you use the official API, a conversation where the customer hasn't written for more than 24 hours can only be restarted with an approved template. Meta explains the customer service window on the official WhatsApp Business Platform pricing page. In practice, keep a template ready for the Quote sent stage.

Pipeline examples by industry

The generic template needs translating for each kind of business. What changes is the fact that marks each move. Here are four examples.

IndustryQualified whenQuote whenWon when
ClinicGave procedure and insurance or self-payGot price and open slotsAppointment booked
Real estateSaid buy or rent, area and budgetViewing scheduledOffer signed
StorePicked model, color or sizeGot total with shippingPayment confirmed
ServicesDescribed the job and deadlineGot a written quoteAccepted and paid deposit

In real estate, the viewing is usually a better stage than sending a price, because the price was already in the listing. In a store, the critical moment is the total with shipping: that's where many people drop off. Every industry has that point where the customer decides, and it deserves its own stage.

A dental clinic might split "Assessment booked" from "Treatment plan sent," because those are two different decisions for the patient. A clothing store probably doesn't need Negotiation at all. Cut whatever doesn't happen in your business.

Lost reasons: the column that teaches you the most

Hardly anyone likes recording a lost sale. But that's where the best information in the pipeline lives. Create a short, closed list of reasons so it doesn't turn into free text you can't add up:

  • Price: found it expensive or bought cheaper elsewhere.
  • Timing: needed it sooner than we could deliver or schedule.
  • No reply: stopped answering after the last follow-up.
  • Not a fit: product, area or service outside what we offer.
  • We were slow: customer said they'd already sorted it by the time we answered.
  • Other: with one line of explanation.

After a month, count them. If "No reply" is the top reason, the problem is your follow-up, and a good set of follow-up message templates will do more than a discount. If "We were slow" shows up often, the pipeline is leaking before it even starts. If it's "Price," your qualification may be letting through people outside your market.

Advice from someone who got this wrong: don't let "Other" become the most used reason. If it does, read those cards and create the reason that's missing.

The three weekly numbers that show where sales leak

With well-defined stages, three simple divisions show where the pipeline is leaking. Do them every Monday, looking at the cards that came in the week before.

Here's a hypothetical example. A custom furniture store got 120 new WhatsApp leads in one week:

MoveCountRate
New to Qualified84 of 12070%
Qualified to Quote30 of 8436%
Quote to Won9 of 3030%

Overall, 9 sales from 120 leads, or 7.5%. That number alone doesn't say much. The three rates do.

Now compare two possible improvements. If the store lifts its quote close rate from 30% to 40%, the same 30 quotes give 12 sales instead of 9. If it instead moves more qualified leads to a quote (from 36% to 45%), it gets about 38 quotes and, still closing 30%, roughly 11 sales.

With a hypothetical average ticket of US$2,000, three extra sales a week is US$6,000. The math makes it obvious where to spend energy first. In this example, Qualified to Quote is the weakest step: 54 people said what they wanted and never got a price. Open those cards and find out why. Often the answer is simple: the quote depends on measuring the room, and nobody booked the visit.

A fourth, optional number: time stuck in a stage. Cards sitting in Quote sent for more than seven days rarely close without a nudge. Where to start this week

Don't redesign the whole pipeline at once. Take the board you already have and write one sentence next to each column name: "a card enters here when...". Show it to the team and ask if anyone disagrees. The ten-minute discussion that follows is worth more than any new tool.

On Friday, clean up: every card without a next step gets a date or goes to Lost with a reason. On Monday, run the three numbers for the first time.

Frequently asked questions

How many stages should a WhatsApp sales pipeline have?

For most small businesses, four to six. Fewer hides where customers get stuck; more and the team stops updating the board.

What is the difference between a sales pipeline and a CRM?

The pipeline is the sequence of stages a customer goes through before buying. The CRM is the tool where you record each customer, their current stage and the next step.

How do I calculate the conversion rate of each stage?

Divide the number of cards that moved to the next stage by the total that entered the stage in the same period. For example, 30 quotes and 9 sales give a 30% quote-to-sale rate.

Should I delete lost customers from the pipeline?

No. Move them to Lost with a reason. That is where you learn whether you lose on price, timing or slow replies, and some of those customers can be contacted again later.
