Customer service metrics that matter: 5 numbers to check every Monday (and the ones to ignore)

Your average response time may be lying to you. Here's how five simple numbers, checked every Monday, show what customers actually experience.

LVBy Lucas Vaz·October 21, 2026·7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Use the median first response time, not the average: one 4-hour case skews everything.
  • Set your SLA from your current number and track the share of conversations within it.
  • Unanswered conversations, counted at the same time every day, are the most honest service alarm.
  • NPS is the percentage of promoters (9 and 10) minus the percentage of detractors (0 to 6), per Bain's method.
  • Total messages sent and database size are vanity metrics: they look good and tell you nothing.

Customer service metrics are one of those topics everyone agrees are important and almost nobody looks at. Or worse: people look at the wrong number. I've seen a store owner celebrate "over 3,000 messages sent this month" while 40 customers had been waiting for a reply since Friday.

The good news is that you don't need a dashboard with twenty charts. Five well-defined numbers, checked on the same day every week, tell you almost everything about how customers are being served on WhatsApp. The rest is noise.

Here they are, with the math worked out step by step.

How to calculate first response time

First response time is the gap between the customer's message and the business's first useful reply. An automatic "we got your message" doesn't count. If you include it, everyone has a zero-second response time and the number becomes meaningless.

Two decisions before you calculate:

  • Hours: measure business hours separately from everything else. Mixing an 11 p.m. message with a 10 a.m. one hides the picture.
  • What counts: an AI reply that answers the question counts as useful. An AI that only says "an agent will be with you shortly" doesn't.

Why the median beats the average

Here's a hypothetical example. A beauty clinic measured first response time for 10 conversations one morning, in minutes:

2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 15 and 240.

The average is the sum divided by 10: 298 ÷ 10 = 29.8 minutes. It looks like every customer waits half an hour.

The median is the middle value once you sort the list. With 10 numbers, it's the average of the 5th and 6th: (5 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5.5 minutes. That's what the typical customer experiences.

And those 240 minutes? Don't make them disappear. A conversation that waited four hours is a real problem, just a different one: someone forgot, or the chat landed on the wrong phone. The median shows what's normal; the list of outliers shows the holes. Look at both.

How to set a realistic SLA

An SLA is your internal promise of a maximum time: "we reply within X minutes during business hours." The most common mistake is copying a nice-looking number from the internet. If your median today is 40 minutes, promising 2 minutes will only demoralize the team.

An approach that works:

  1. Measure your current median for two weeks.
  2. Set a target around half of that, but achievable with the team you have.
  3. Track the percentage of conversations within the SLA, not just the median.
  4. Review after a month.

In the clinic example, with a 10-minute SLA, seven of the ten conversations made it (2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8). That's 70% within SLA, a number the team understands and can improve.

Unanswered conversations: the most honest alarm

If I could pick just one number to watch, it would be this: how many conversations end with a customer message and no reply from the business.

It doesn't depend on averages or medians. You can't dress it up. And it points straight at the customer who is waiting right now.

To compare day to day, always count at the same time, for example 9 a.m. and 5 p.m. Set a threshold too: conversations unanswered for more than 30 minutes during business hours. A list at zero by 5 p.m. is the best end-of-day feeling a service manager can have.

One detail: a customer's closing "Thanks!" doesn't need a reply. Close those conversations so they don't clutter the list.

Resolution rate: resolved for whom?

Resolution rate is the percentage of conversations that end with the customer's problem solved. Sounds obvious, but the hard part is defining "solved."

The rule I use: a conversation is resolved when it's closed and the customer doesn't come back about the same issue within seven days. If they come back, it was reopened and doesn't count.

A hypothetical example: in one week, an online store closed 200 conversations. Of those, 26 came back with the same issue ("my order still hasn't arrived," "the coupon didn't work"). Resolution rate: (200 − 26) ÷ 200 = 87%.

The 13% that came back are a gold mine. Read those conversations. Two or three repeated reasons almost always show up, and each one becomes a better saved reply, new knowledge for the AI or a process fix.

What NPS is and how to calculate it

The Net Promoter Score was introduced by Fred Reichheld of Bain & Company in a 2003 Harvard Business Review article. The idea is to measure loyalty with a single question:

On a scale of 0 to 10, how likely are you to recommend [company name] to a friend or colleague?

According to Bain's own explanation, people who answer 9 or 10 are promoters, 7 or 8 are passives, and 0 to 6 are detractors. NPS is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors, so it ranges from −100 to +100.

Let's run the math on a hypothetical set of 50 responses:

GroupScoresResponses%
Promoters9 and 103060%
Passives7 and 81224%
Detractors0 to 6816%

NPS = 60 − 16 = 44. Passives count in the total but not in the subtraction.

Three practical cautions. Ask right after the conversation, while the 24-hour window is still open, and only once per resolved conversation. Always ask for an optional comment ("What could we do better?"), because that's what tells you what to change. And with only a few dozen responses the score jumps around: watch the trend over a few months, not the week.

Hi Emma! It's Paula from Bright Smile Dental. Any questions left about your appointment?

If you have a second, reply with a number from 0 to 10: how likely are you to recommend us to someone? It takes 5 seconds and helps a lot.

Vanity metrics you can ignore

Some numbers look great in a meeting and don't change a single decision. The main ones:

  • Total messages sent. It can go up because the team is serving more people, or because it took five messages to explain what one well-written message would have covered.
  • Total contacts in your database. It tells you how many you've collected, not how many still buy or read.
  • Campaign read rate on its own. Reading isn't replying or buying.
  • Average handle time as a target. If the team is pushed to close fast, they'll close fast, solved or not. Use it to diagnose, not as a goal.

A simple test: if this number went up 20% tomorrow, what would you do differently? If the answer is "nothing," it's vanity.

Start with the Monday 9 a.m. list

Don't try to measure all five at once. Next Monday at 9 a.m., count only the unanswered conversations. Write it down. Do it again at 5 p.m. Repeat all week.

In five days you'll know at what time the queue grows and who usually clears it. The following week, add the median response time. Within a month the dashboard is complete, and the team is used to looking at it without feeling policed.

That last part matters more than it seems. A metric that becomes a scolding becomes a fudged number. A metric that becomes a team conversation becomes an improvement.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between first response time and average handle time?

First response time is how long the customer waits for the first useful reply. Average handle time is how long the whole conversation takes until it's resolved. The first measures waiting; the second, effort.

How do you calculate NPS?

Ask customers, on a 0 to 10 scale, how likely they are to recommend you. Count those who gave 9 or 10 (promoters) and 0 to 6 (detractors). NPS is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors.

What is a good customer service SLA on WhatsApp?

It depends on your business and team. Start from your current time, set a slightly better and achievable target for business hours and track the share of conversations within it. Use a different rule after hours.

How many responses do I need for NPS to be meaningful?

With only a few dozen responses the score swings a lot from week to week. Watch the trend over several months and read the comments, which usually teach more than the score.
