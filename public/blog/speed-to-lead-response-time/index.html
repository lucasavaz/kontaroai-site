Speed to lead: why the first hour is worth more than the rest of the week

A study of 1.25 million leads found that waiting just one extra hour sharply cuts your odds of a real conversation. Here is what that means if you sell on WhatsApp, and how to build a rule your team can actually keep.

LVBy Lucas Vaz·November 11, 2026·8 min read

Key takeaways

  • In the study published in HBR, firms that tried to reach a lead within an hour were nearly 7 times as likely to qualify it as firms that tried an hour later.
  • A lead is not a customer yet: they asked you for a price, and probably two other businesses in the same minute.
  • A 5-minute rule doesn't need heroes. It needs a named owner for every time slot.
  • The first reply has to be useful, not just “we got your message”.
  • Track response time for leads separately from the rest of your support.

Friday, 6:40 p.m. Someone sees an ad for a custom cabinet shop, taps through and writes: “How much for 10 feet of kitchen cabinets?”. The shop replies Monday at 9 a.m. with a carefully prepared quote. By then the customer had already signed with another shop on Saturday morning.

Nobody was rude. Nobody got the price wrong. The problem was speed to lead, and it costs more than it looks because the loss is invisible: a sale that never happened doesn't show up in any report.

This piece is about leads: people who asked for a price, a quote or information and haven't bought anything yet. For the day-to-day routine of answering everyone faster, we have a separate guide on how to answer WhatsApp customers faster. Here the topic is narrower: the first hour of a lead's life.

What the Harvard Business Review study actually found

In 2011, three researchers published a short piece in Harvard Business Review called “The Short Life of Online Sales Leads”. It gets quoted everywhere, usually with the wrong numbers, so it's worth reading what the original text says.

They did two things. First, they sent a test web lead to 2,241 US companies and timed the response:

  • 37% responded within an hour;
  • 16% responded within one to 24 hours;
  • 24% took more than 24 hours;
  • 23% never responded at all.

Among the companies that responded within 30 days, the average response time was 42 hours.

The second part is the one that matters. In a separate study of 1.25 million leads received by 42 companies, firms that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it (have a meaningful conversation with a decision maker) as firms that tried even an hour later. And more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited 24 hours or longer.

Two honest caveats. The study is American, from 2011, and contact was mostly by phone. You'll also see figures like “100 times” or “21 times” attributed to this research online: they aren't in the original piece. Even with those caveats, the direction is clear, and it matches what any salesperson has seen firsthand.

Why leads go cold so fast on WhatsApp

People asking for a quote on WhatsApp almost never ask just one business. They open three or four chats in the same minute, send the same question and wait. The first useful answer becomes the benchmark. The others get compared to it, if they get read at all.

There's another detail: interest has a short shelf life. The customer was thinking about cabinets at 6:40 p.m. because they had just measured the kitchen. At 9 p.m. they're having dinner. On Saturday they're at a kid's soccer game. By Monday the topic has lost its urgency, or it's already solved.

That's why a lead is different from an existing customer with a question. The customer will wait a bit. The lead owes you nothing.

What does a slow reply cost? A worked example with hypothetical numbers

The numbers below are a hypothetical example, just to show the reasoning. Swap in your own.

Picture a custom cabinet shop that gets 80 leads a month from Instagram ads. It pays about US$12 per lead. The average project is US$4,500.

ScenarioLeadsClose rateSales
Reply in minutes808%6.4
Reply next day804%3.2

That's a gap of 3.2 projects a month. Times US$4,500, about US$14,400 in sales that never happened. Ad spend is identical in both scenarios: US$960. In the second one, the shop paid to create demand and handed half of it to the competition.

Notice I didn't use the study's “seven times”. I used a drop by half, which is far more conservative, and the math is already uncomfortable. Run your own version with real data: take last month's leads, separate the ones answered in under 15 minutes from the rest, and compare how many closed in each group.

How to set up a 5-minute rule your team can keep

Five minutes is not a magic number from the research. It's a practical target: short enough that you're first most of the time, long enough that a person can hit it without dropping everything.

The rule only works if it answers three questions before anyone needs it.

1. Who owns leads in each time slot?

“The team answers” is not a rule. Write names by time slot: 8 to 12, Anna; 12 to 2, Ben covers lunch; 2 to 6, Anna again. After hours, who or what replies? If the answer is “nobody”, you already know where your lost leads are.

2. How do leads stand out from everything else?

If a quote request lands in the middle of 40 chats from existing customers asking for an invoice copy, it will wait. Tag leads with a label or a “new lead” stage and keep that queue visible at all times.

3. What must the first reply contain?

A fast but empty reply (“Hi! We got your message and will get back to you soon”) only confirms that nobody is looking. The first reply has to move the conversation: a price, a price range, a qualifying question or a proposed time.

What to write in the first message

The best first reply does two things: it gives something concrete and ends with an easy question. Three templates to adapt.

When you can share a price range right away:

Hi Sarah, it's Anna from Pinewood Cabinets. Ten feet of kitchen cabinets usually runs between US$3,800 and US$5,500, depending on the finish.

Do you already have the wall measurements, or would you like us to come measure?

When the price depends on missing information:

Hi Mark! I can get you a price today. I just need two things: your car model, and whether you want tint on the side windows only or the whole car.

When the next step is a visit or assessment:

Hi Julia! The assessment is free and takes about 30 minutes. I have tomorrow at 10 a.m. or Thursday at 4 p.m. Does either work for you?

Notice what's missing: “Dear customer”, “Thank you for reaching out”, a link to the full catalog. Every extra line is one less line of attention for what matters.

What about leads that arrive when nobody is watching?

For many businesses, a good share of requests arrive after hours: at night, on Sunday, over lunch. You have three options, from worst to best:

  1. Silence until the next day. It's the default, and it's the 42-hour scenario from the study.
  2. An away message. Better than nothing, as long as it says when someone will reply and already asks the qualifying question, so the customer can answer in the meantime.
  3. An AI that actually answers. Trained on your price list and policies, it gives the price range, handles simple questions, offers time slots and leaves the conversation ready for the team in the morning.

How to tell if the rule is working

You only need two numbers, checked every Monday:

  • Time to first useful reply, for leads only. Use the median, not the average: one lead forgotten over the weekend skews the average and hides whether the week was good (or bad).
  • Close rate by response time. How many leads answered within 5 minutes became sales, compared with those answered after an hour.

The second number is the one that convinces the team. When salespeople see in their own spreadsheet that the leads they answered quickly close more often, nobody needs a pep talk.

Start tomorrow with a sheet of paper

Before changing any tool, run a simple test. Tomorrow, write down by hand the time each quote request arrived and the time of the first useful reply. Just that, for one day.

At the end of the day, look at the list. If most replies came in under 5 minutes, great, your bottleneck is somewhere else. If you see two- or three-hour waits in the middle of the afternoon, you just found money walking out the back door. And now you know exactly what time it leaves.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good lead response time?

As close to instant as you can get. A practical target is under 5 minutes during business hours, and never more than an hour without a useful reply, even at night.

Does an auto-reply count as responding to a lead?

Only if it moves things forward. A message that just says “we received your request” won't hold anyone. One that gives a price range, asks the right question or offers a time slot does count.

Does the HBR study apply to WhatsApp?

It's from 2011, with US companies and web leads contacted mostly by phone. The channel has changed, but the behavior of people who ask several businesses for a quote at once has not.

How do I know how much slow replies are costing me?

Split last month's leads into two groups: answered in under 15 minutes and answered later. Compare how many became sales in each group. The gap times your average sale is a rough estimate of the loss.
